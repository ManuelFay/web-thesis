\chapter{Perspectives on Search}

\label{ch:conclusion}

Vannevar Bush had the vision that searching using \emph{associative} links would be the key insight to unlock new retrieval capabilities \cite{bush1945aswemaythink}. Time proved him right, well aided by the advent of computing. Statistical retrieval engines revolutionized fixed classification systems by linking information queries to documents through their words, not just their general topic. Deep neural networks further strengthened these associative links by moving beyond surface-level syntax and instead exploiting semantic proximity. The contributions of this thesis could be seen as further efforts to improve \emph{search} by exploring the extent to which we can create associative links.

At a high level, with \emph{Visual Document Retrieval} \sidecite{faysse2025colpaliefficientdocumentretrieval, loison2026vidorev3comprehensiveevaluation, teiletche2025modernvbert}, we demonstrate that cross-modal links can be leveraged to efficiently match query terms to image parts. In our work on contextual embeddings \sidecite{conti2025context}, we show existing structural links \emph{between} corpus documents can and should be exploited to facilitate retrieval. Through Large Language Model pretraining projects \sidecite{faysse_croissantllm_2024, meeus2024copyright}, we explore parametric memory consolidation, and study which type of knowledge can be surfaced (searched) from the LLM memory through prompting (associative recall). Associative links come in many forms. This thesis explores only a subset of them, but aims to show that broadening the types of links used for retrieval can open fruitful research directions. In particular, I believe that many promising directions remain underexplored beyond the beaten (but nevertheless useful) path of improving text embeddings primarily through larger contrastive datasets and larger models.

Future research directions in line with this vision are further detailed.

\section{The Associative Memory Hierarchy Perspective}

Viewing \emph{search} solely through the lens of document retrieval is restrictive; a broader perspective is to understand search as \emph{memory access} across a hierarchy of storage mediums. Humans answer questions multiple ways: by relying on internal memory, by attending to information currently in view (consulting notes), or by looking up information online or in a library. All of these memory systems differ in capacity, latency, reliability, update cost, and provenance. As mapped in Table \ref{tab:mem-hiera}, LLM systems exhibit an analogous hierarchy.

Model weights act as compressed parametric memory; the current context, together with the key-value cache, provides a transient working memory; and document stores, databases, tools, or the web provide external non-parametric memory (Table \ref{tab:mem-hiera}). \sidenote{This analogy is strengthened by recent work interpreting Transformers through the lens of associative memory. In particular, \cite{zhong2025understandingtransformerperspectiveassociative} argues that both attention and feed-forward networks can be understood as memory systems, but with distinct retrieval and update properties. Attention constructs a dynamic associative memory over the current sequence, whereas feed-forward layers behave more like static learned associative stores.}

\noindent\textbf{Retrieval and Indexing.} Under this view, standard document retrieval (for RAG usecases for instance) is simply the promotion of information from external memory into working memory. Similarly, LLM prompting and autoregressive generation is the retrieval mechanism that moves information from parametric memory to the working memory. Within the working memory, the attention operation performs in-context retrieval using learned associative links between query and key representations (which could be seen as indexes). To continue with the analogy, standard \emph{document indexing} stores information from the working memory into external memory. Language Modeling through backpropagation inversely consolidates this information into the model weights, a sort of in-weights index triggered by future relevant activations.  

This framework is useful in the sense where it allows to draw links between fields that today are mostly disjoint. My view is that some of the most interesting research problems in retrieval lie at their intersection. Work such as the Differentiable Search Index \sidecite{tay2022transformermemorydifferentiablesearch} explicitly learn a corpus index in parametric memory. Instead of creating document embeddings through a model forward pass, work such as \sidecite{eyuboglu2025cartridgeslightweightgeneralpurposelong, kuratov2025cramming1568tokenssingle} optimize the representation through backpropagation, inspired by the prefix tuning approach \sidecite{li2021prefixtuningoptimizingcontinuousprompts}.  Late Interaction \sidecite{khattab2020colbert} stems from the concept of better simulating the attention operation to allow for a matching operation more expressive than dot product similarity. Finally, modern agentic search systems achieve success in my view thanks to their ability to leverage all forms of memory jointly (parametric, in-context, external). These systems can even be reinforced by erasing the distinction between in-context memory and external memory with works like Recursive Language Models \sidecite{zhang2026recursivelanguagemodels}.

Breaking down artificial preconceptions and unifying the document retrieval field with the broader ideas from associative memory research is a powerful avenue for future improvement. Very concretely, long-context sparse attention mechanisms from million-token context windows are inches away from document retrieval over external memories: both fields study how to represent information and index it as dense embeddings, such that further associative recall is triggered given a relevant query. In \emph{Self-Pruned Key Value Attention: Learning When To Write by Predicting Future Utility} \sidecite{szilvasy2026selfprunedkeyvalueattentionlearning}, we devise ways to index information in a LLM KV Cache, but selectively learn when such information bears no signal and should not be persisted in memory. Such mechanisms would directly benefit most document retrieval systems.

\noindent\textbf{Research Idea.} Given more time, an idea I would have loved to explore is learning document embeddings, solely through language modeling loss on unsupervised data. To do so, block sparse attention can be used to selectively include certain text sequences within the model training window, (softly) conditionned on the block's representation. Ideally, the block representation would be learned implicitly through soft gating techniques, similar to those used in our Self-Pruned KV paper. Blocks whose inclusion improves the language modeling objective would be assigned representations that make them more likely to be included, while unhelpful blocks would be filtered out. While it is unclear whether such an approach could outperform modern contrastive learning methods trained on supervised retrieval data, it would offer a different and potentially scalable route to document representation learning. In particular, it would make it possible to train retrieval-oriented representations on internet-scale corpora using only the language modeling objective, without relying on explicit supervision signals.

We argue that \emph{omnimodal} search will become central as the set of exploitable sources of information widens with model capabilites (text, images, audio, video, code, structured data, 3D models).
Recent projects such as BrowseComp illustrate the growing impact of AI agents on real-world information seeking and highlight that robust access to complex information benefits from \emph{adaptive compute}. Crucially, these systems still benefit from strong ``first-layer'' retrieval primitives such as embedding-based search for efficient candidate selection.


We further note that not all information is equally accessible: some sources remain costly to reach, poorly indexed, private, ephemeral... Exploiting such information at scale will therefore remain challenging in the coming years. Looking forward, we anticipate a shift from explicit querying toward more \emph{implicit} information access, where systems proactively surface relevant knowledge continuously in response to context, goals, and ongoing activity.

SCAFFOLDING ENGINEERING AND OVERFITIINGG ON BENVHMARKS

To improve systems, we need to have measurable progress and be able to verify search system performance at scale  (``verifier's law.'') At large scale, actors such as Google and OpenAi can use weak supervision signals to evaluate and improve their systems---such as reformulations, follow-up queries, dwell time, and abandonment---which provide implicit evidence of whether the user's need has actually been satisfied.  In academaia and for small actors, other ways are needed to obtain evaluation signal at scale.

As model intelligence becomes increasingly commoditized, the model performance bottleneck shifts from leveraging known information through reasoning and generation, to simply obtaining and accessing relevant information to condition the model, ie. \emph{context engineering}. This bottleneck is already visible in retrieval-augmented generation, where system performance is often limited less by the generator than by the quality of the retrieved context. Extrapolating forward, one can imagine models becoming near-optimal decision-makers in many domains, provided they are given sufficiently rich, relevant, and reliable evidence. On this view, the core challenge is no longer merely to generate well, but to construct the right informational substrate on which generation and reasoning can operate. Prediction markets offer a particularly useful testbed for these ideas because they combine downstream utility with eventual verifiability. They reward agents that can exploit information asymmetries when market prices diverge from well-calibrated beliefs, whether this advantage comes from better reasoning, better calibration, or better access to relevant information. Because outcomes ultimately resolve, performance can be measured in an actionable and externally grounded way. For this reason, prediction markets may become a valuable proxy for progress in agentic search: they test not only whether a system can retrieve information, but whether it can turn retrieval into decisions that demonstrably create value. In such a future, AI agents may act both as market participants and as mechanisms for rapidly incorporating public information into calibrated probabilities, thereby increasing the premium on private information and on search systems able to go deeper or move faster than the market.

\section*{Search On}

Intelligence is becoming a commodity, sold to consumers by trillion-dollar companies. Blindly trusting state-of-the-art LLM outputs is becoming an almost rational decision: as we improve modeling and search tooling, model responses are most often going to be correct and well grounded in verifiable sources.

\emph{Searching} however, goes beyond just getting an answer to a specific question; it requires understanding it. To do so, one must often first build  an understanding of several prerequisite topics. One must explore, reason, backtrack, synthesize. We search by exploring the maze of human knowledge, but the path is beautiful and interesting; it leads through side quests and down rabbit holes. The search for a good outdoors camera leads to discovering the competitive birdwatching community. Questions about why the brick road is yellow in the \emph{Wizard of Oz}, are a surprisingly few amounts of logical clicks away from reading all about the Opium wars.

Searching is the gateway to knowledge, but the path stimulates curiosity and bootstraps creativity. Shiny search tools are invaluable to progress as a society, and search agents are clearly the future. Sometimes however, aimlessly digging through the disorganized pile of parchments in the library of Alexandria is still the best option.
