\section{This Thesis in a Glance} 


Deep neural networks used to index and query information rely on a semantic understanding of the content to retrieve documents\sidenote{A \textbf{document} is an information unit of limited size, typically small enough for a human reader to assess its relevance to a query, such as a page, paragraph, or passage. Although documents are ideally self-contained, they often depend on surrounding context; for example, a page in a textbook may contain the sought-after formula while requiring preceding pages for proper interpretation.} at large scales. This contrasts with previous methods that leveraged meta-information (title, author, tags, document visits), or relied on statistical heuristics to match content to a user query (keyword matching). 

More precisely, efforts in deep semantic search have to this day mostly focused on creating meaningful latent representation spaces to map short text passages to their equivalent latent representations ("embeddings") in a manifold that aligns semantical proximity with geometric distance. This thesis argues that in practice, solely viewing AI search through this prism is quite restrictive: search systems should move beyond standard text embeddings to perform document retrieval. The thesis explores two main improvement directions which share one common principle: leveraging easily available yet often overlooked information ("priors"). Importantly, it does so in a way that can scale to billions of documents, and such that future improvements of LLM capabilities should only further strengthen these contributions \sidecite{sutton2019bitter}.

\subsection{Part 1: Understanding Documents Requires Vision}


Historically, AI search engines have treated documents primarily as text. This thesis introduces the concept of \textit{Visual Document Retrieval} (VDR) through the publication ``ColPali: Efficient Document Retrieval with Vision Language Models'' \sidecite{faysse2025colpaliefficientdocumentretrieval}. This work proposes performing document retrieval without relying on text extracted from document pages. Instead, pages are indexed directly from their images, allowing the retrieval system to capture the semantic content expressed through pixels, including text, layout, tables, figures, and other visual elements.

We show that this approach substantially simplifies and accelerates document indexing pipelines, while also improving retrieval performance, particularly for visually complex documents with rich layouts, tables, figures, or other non-linear structures.

The success of VDR has led since the original publication to many follow up works by academic and industrial actors. As a result, model performances rapidly improved, saturating the original Visual Document Retrieval benchmark \textit{ViDoRe} introduced in the ColPali paper, and leading to subsequent benchmarking efforts mentioned rapidly in this thesis in chapter XXX. In ViDoRe V2 \sidecite{macé2025vidorebenchmarkv2raising}, we construct complex and realistic pairs of queries and documents to assess retrieval performance on open-ended questions spanning up to multiple pages and rely on complex AI pipelines to simulate realistic interactions between users and information corpora. In ViDoRe V3 \sidecite{loison2026vidorev3comprehensiveevaluation}, we collaborate with Nvidia, to further scale annotation efforts on varied domains reflecting real world use of industrial search systems and develop an annotation pipeline that combines AI agents with over 12000 hours of expert human annotators.

A year after the publication of the ColPali paper, we released \emph{ModernVBert}, a visual document retrieval model that commoditizes VDR performance by showcasing similar performances than the original ColPali model while being 10x smaller and running on CPU \sidecite{teiletche2025modernvbert}. This was made possible by through ablations of all performance factors behind vision language retrieval models and many critical insights were uncovered and detailed in chapter XX.

The contributions of this thesis go beyond a series of peer reviewed publications. Significant efforts were devoted to continuously improve the performance, to extend the capabilities and to advocate for the use of Visual Document Retrieval models. We can notably mention the release of the ColQwen model series\sidenote{We published the models openly and integrated the code on HuggingFace \url{https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/main/model_doc/colqwen2}}, a series of SOTA visual retrievers, downloaded over 5 million times on HuggingFace at the time of writing. We also extended retrieval to other modalities such as video and audio through ColQwen-Omni \sidenote{We published the model openly on the HuggingFace platform and released a blogpost at \url{https://huggingface.co/blog/manu/colqwen-omni-omnimodal-retrieval}.} and maintained the ColPali GitHub library at \url{https://github.com/illuin-tech/colpali}. The field of Visual Document Retrieval is now well established and thriving.



\subsection{Part 2: Understanding Documents Requires Context}

Another limit of standard embedding based search is the fact "documents" are processed in isolation. Understanding a paragraph from the middle of a book is a tall ask when previous pages have never been read...
 A limitation of Visual Document Retrieval models are their limited ability to leverage contextual information found in previous document pages. 
This shortcoming is in fact not inherent to visual retrievers, but is also a major weakness of the more common textual embedding models which embed long documents as a series of independant passages, without exploiting the fact to a reader, certain passages might be key to understand others. We quantify the impact of context aware retrieval and propose technical solutions based on late-chunking aware training in chapter X \sidecite{conti2025context}. While this work is instanciated on textual retrievers for ease of experimentation, it is directly applicable to our work on improving visual retrievers.


The lack of contextualization does not lie solely on the document side. To be properly parsed, queries also require context (what does the user really look for), and often require multiple steps of reasoning and prior knowledge to be properly processed (to search for the GDP of Usain's Bolt's country, you first need to know or search for Bolt's nationality, then search for updated information about Jamaica's GDP). Standard embedding based search systems would be mostly incapable of such multi-step reasoning, hence we turn to the most recent evolution of search engines: AI agents powered by Large Language Models (LLMs): systems in which a language model acts as an orchestrator, decomposing a user request into intermediate steps, issuing retrieval or tool-use actions, and integrating the results into a final answer.
Recent efforts such as BrowseComp highlight the growing importance of AI agents for real-world information seeking, and suggest that robust access to practically useful information increasingly benefits from \emph{adaptive compute}. Importantly, these systems continue to depend on strong first-layer retrieval primitives---notably embedding-based search---to identify promising candidates before deeper reasoning, verification, or tool use can take place, and should be seen as a complementary abstraction.


Agentic search largely benefits from capable LLM orchestrators. More capable models are infused with better reasoning skills and better background knowledge enabling a more precise use of retrieval tools and are able to retrieve information quicker and in less turns (BrwoseCompPlus). While the best LLMs have historically been proprietary models of large size, essentially pretrained on the english language, we argue smaller models trained openly and transparently are capable of strong performance and benefit the academic and industrial community. In Chapter~X, we introduce CroissantLLM \sidecite{faysse2024croissantllm}, a compact bilingual French--English language model trained on an equal mixture of English and French data. Initiated in 2023, this project was among the first open efforts to highlight the benefits of training with high token-to-parameter ratios, and among the early attempts to train sub-7B models \emph{outside} of a larger scaling ladder with the explicit goal of delivering strong performance at small size for efficient inference. At release time, CroissantLLM achieved state-of-the-art results on French language benchmarks and matched the strongest English models in its size category. It also delivered particularly strong performance on English--French translation, often surpassing models that were orders of magnitude larger. Beyond the model itself, the CroissantLLM project emphasizes the importance of bilingual training corpora, establishes joint scaling laws for bilingual settings, and is accompanied by substantial data work: the release of high-quality French resources reused in many subsequent projects, as well as new French benchmarks. Models, code, datasets, and evaluation suites are released publicly, supporting continued progress toward strong local models that run efficiently on consumer hardware.


% Finally, a line of work conducted as part of this thesis addresses the transparency problem from a more adversarial perspective. Led primarily by the Computational Privacy Group at Imperial College London, the two papers are discussed only briefly in Chapter~X. They focus on \emph{training-data memorization} and \emph{membership inference} in large language models: to what extent are individual training examples memorized, and how can one provide evidence that a given text was included in an LLM's training corpus? These questions have direct relevance to ongoing legal disputes and raise broad policy implications, notably around copyright and data governance. In \sidecite{meeus2024copyright}, we propose inserting a small fraction of controlled ``canaries'' into the training data in order to reliably characterize the regimes in which memorization occurs and to quantify the associated risk of information leakage. This work is done through the CroissantLLM training project. Complementarily, \sidecite{meeus2024sok} provides a systematic  overview of membership inference attacks against LLM training data, organizing existing methods, clarifying threat models and assumptions, and highlighting open problems. Together, these contributions move toward more rigorous and auditable assessments of modern LLM behavior, and offer practical tools to reason about privacy and provenance in large-scale training pipelines.

At its core, indexing and retrieving information from external sources in a working "memory" is likely to remain one of the key challenges of AI systems in the future. Scientific advances will unlock better recall from broader sources and more personalized answers, so the form factor will change (agentic scaffold, memory), but the need to condition a generative process (our decision making, a generative LLM for RAG) on verifiable non-parametric sources remains essential to ground and interpret practical decisions with tractable ressources.  


\noindent\textbf{Omni-modality.} We are moving from predominantly text-based retrieval toward a more \emph{omnimodal} regime, in which useful evidence may reside in text, images, audio, video, code, structured data, and potentially even 3D assets. This shift was bootstrapped by systems such as ColPali, but since reflects a broader evolution of the field. As AI-powered search becomes more capable, the competitive edge of a system will increasingly depend on its ability to access and exploit heterogeneous sources. Not all information, however, is equally reachable: some content will remain costly to access, poorly indexed, private, ephemeral, or expressed in unfamiliar modalities. The ability to retrieve and operationalize such information at scale may therefore become one of the main factors separating merely good systems from truly exceptional ones. Gemini, MixedBread

\noindent\textbf{Beyond Single Vector Search.} Late Interaction, Memory, Agentic Scaffolds, RLMs. 
Memories: Finally, long-context modeling and information retrieval may be closer than they first appear. Both fields are fundamentally concerned with how to select, compress, and condition generation on relevant prior evidence---that is, how to find useful signals within overwhelming amounts of noise. Building stronger conceptual and methodological links between these literatures may therefore prove especially fruitful. More broadly, the future of search may not lie in replacing retrieval with larger models, but in understanding how retrieval, long-context reasoning, and adaptive computation can be combined into a unified theory of information access.



\noindent\textbf{Measuring Progress.} A central constraint on future progress is that search quality is not uniformly easy to measure and verify. Search appears to follow a \emph{jagged frontier} of capabilities: some tasks, such as factual verification grounded in accessible digital sources, are comparatively straightforward to check and should improve steadily,\sidenote{However, as demonstrated by Vidore V3, even ostensibly well-defined retrieval tasks can remain extremely time-consuming to verify for human experts.} whereas underspecified requests, open-ended synthesis, and value-laden questions remain difficult both to solve and to evaluate. Under this lens, verifier-side constraints help determine which classes of search problems can be improved reliably under something like ``verifier's law.'' Strong benchmarks therefore remain essential for hill-climbing in the near term, but ultimately downstream utility is the more meaningful measure. In practice, modern systems also benefit from abundant weak supervision signals---such as reformulations, follow-up queries, dwell time, and abandonment---which provide implicit evidence of whether the user's need has actually been satisfied.

\noindent\textbf{The information edge.}  As model intelligence becomes increasingly commoditized, the model performance bottleneck shifts from leveraging known information through reasoning and generation, to simply obtaining and accessing relevant information to condition the model, ie. \emph{context engineering}. This bottleneck is already visible in retrieval-augmented generation, where system performance is often limited less by the generator than by the quality of the retrieved context. Extrapolating forward, one can imagine models becoming near-optimal decision-makers in many domains, provided they are given sufficiently rich, relevant, and reliable evidence. On this view, the core challenge is no longer merely to generate well, but to construct the right informational substrate on which generation and reasoning can operate. Prediction markets offer a particularly useful testbed for these ideas because they combine downstream utility with eventual verifiability. They reward agents that can exploit information asymmetries when market prices diverge from well-calibrated beliefs, whether this advantage comes from better reasoning, better calibration, or better access to relevant information. Because outcomes ultimately resolve, performance can be measured in an actionable and externally grounded way. For this reason, prediction markets may become a valuable proxy for progress in agentic search: they test not only whether a system can retrieve information, but whether it can turn retrieval into decisions that demonstrably create value. In such a future, AI agents may act both as market participants and as mechanisms for rapidly incorporating public information into calibrated probabilities, thereby increasing the premium on private information and on search systems able to go deeper or move faster than the market.

Long before computers, humans developed methods to organize and retrieve information. One of the earliest examples is the Pinakes of Callimachus around the 3rd century BCE, often considered the first library catalog in the Western world. The Pinakes systematically listed authors and their works in the Library of Alexandria, grouping works by subject and alphabetizing authors – a bibliographic approach that laid the groundwork for later library catalogs. This ancient effort demonstrated the enduring principles of indexing and categorization (such as alphabetical order and subject grouping) that remain fundamental in information retrieval (IR).


Through the centuries, librarians and scholars continued to develop catalogs and classification systems (e.g. medieval manuscript indexes and Melvil Dewey’s Decimal Classification in 1876 \sidecite{dewey1876classification}). These pre-digital innovations weren’t "IR systems" in the modern sense, but they addressed the same core challenge: how to store, organize, and find relevant information efficiently. By the early 20th century, visionaries like Paul Otlet dreamed of organizing the world’s knowledge (Otlet’s Mundaneum project \sidecite{otlet1928mundaneum}) using index cards – a proto-IR system. In 1945, Vannevar Bush articulated a futuristic vision of personal information devices in As We May Think \sidecite{bush1945aswemaythink}, describing the Memex – a hypothetical electromechanical desk that could instantly retrieve microfilmed documents via associative links. Bush’s essay inspired later developments by suggesting that managing the “growing mountain of information” would require new mechanized solutions.

\section{Founding of Information Retrieval (1950s–1960s)}
IR as a distinct field took shape in the 1950s and 1960s, alongside the advent of digital computers. In 1950, Calvin Mooers coined the term "information retrieval" \cite{mooers1950information}, heralding the idea of using machines to search document collections. Early experiments followed quickly: in 1951, a master’s thesis at MIT by Philip Bagley \sidecite{bagley1951electronic} demonstrated one of the first computerized document retrieval experiments. Pioneers like Hans Peter Luhn at IBM began applying computers to automate indexing; for example, Luhn developed methods for auto-encoding documents and keyword-in-context indexing. By 1959 he published an influential paper on “Auto-encoding of documents for information retrieval” \sidecite{luhn1959autoencoding} showing how computers could encode text and allow electronic searching of content. This period also saw growing support for IR research as the U.S. government recognized an information explosion funding projects on mechanized literature searching and the creation of citation indexes.


A key milestone was the Cranfield experiments led by C. W. “Pete” Cleverdon in the late 1950s and early 1960s \sidecite{cleverdon1967cranfield}. The Cranfield studies introduced a rigorous experimental methodology for IR: assembling a test collection of documents, queries, and relevance judgments, and evaluating retrieval performance using metrics like precision and recall. Cleverdon’s 1962 report on Cranfield showed how different indexing techniques could be objectively compared. This methodology – the use of standardized test collections and metrics – became a cornerstone of IR research, establishing a scientific approach to evaluate how “relevant” the results returned by an IR system are, laying the foundation for future benchmarks.
% The Cranfield approach was hard work (manually judging relevance for many query-document pairs), but it laid the foundation for future benchmarks.


During the same era, Gerard Salton formed the first major IR research group (at Harvard, later Cornell). Researchers began formulating the first theoretical models of IR. In 1960, Maron and Kuhns proposed a probabilistic indexing model \sidecite{maron1960relevance}, foreshadowing later probabilistic retrieval approaches. Salton and others started developing what became the vector space model: by the late 1960s, Salton’s team had created the SMART system, representing documents and queries as vectors in a term-weighted space. In 1968 Salton published “Automatic Information Organization and Retrieval” \sidecite{salton1968automatic} and by 1975 he fully articulated the vector space framework and term-weighting schemes \sidecite{salton1975vector_space}. Another 1960s contribution came from Karen Spärck Jones, who introduced the concept of inverse document frequency (IDF) \cite{sparckjones1972termspecificity} to statistically downweight common terms and boost rare, informative terms – a principle that soon became central to ranking algorithms.

\section{The Statistical Revolution in IR (1970s--1990s)}

With the fundamentals laid, subsequent decades saw rapid improvement in statistical text retrieval methods. The 1970s refined the vector space and probabilistic models. Term frequency--inverse document frequency (TF--IDF) weighting became a standard technique: terms are scored higher if they appear frequently in a document (term frequency) but are rare across the collection (high IDF)\sidecite{sparckjones1972termspecificity}\sidenote{
\textbf{TF-IDF:}
Let $\mathcal{D}=\{d_1,\dots,d_N\}$ be a corpus of $N$ documents and let $t$ be a term (token).
The TF--IDF weight of term $t$ in document $d$ is
\[
\mathrm{tfidf}(t,d,\mathcal{D}) \;=\; \mathrm{tf}(t,d)\cdot \mathrm{idf}(t,\mathcal{D}).
\]
A common choice is
\[
\mathrm{tf}(t,d)=\frac{f_{t,d}}{\sum_{t'} f_{t',d}}
\]
and
\[
\mathrm{idf}(t,\mathcal{D})=\log\!\left(\frac{N}{\mathrm{df}(t)}\right),
\]
where $f_{t,d}$ is the raw count of occurrences of $t$ in $d$, $\sum_{t'} f_{t',d}$ is the total number of term occurrences in $d$ (so $\mathrm{tf}$ is a normalized frequency),
and $\mathrm{df}(t)=\bigl|\{d_i\in\mathcal{D}: t\in d_i\}\bigr|$ is the document frequency (the number of documents containing $t$ at least once).
Intuitively, $\mathrm{tf}$ upweights terms frequent in a given document, while $\mathrm{idf}$ downweights terms that appear in many documents.
(Variants often use $\mathrm{tf}(t,d)=1+\log f_{t,d}$ and/or smoothing $\log\!\left(\frac{N+1}{\mathrm{df}(t)+1}\right)+1$ to avoid division by zero.)
}.  On the probabilistic side, Stephen Robertson and colleagues extended early ideas into the Probabilistic Relevance Framework in the 1970s and 1980s, leading to the development of ranking functions that estimate the probability a document is relevant to a query \sidecite{robertson1976relevance_weighting,robertson1977prp,robertson2009prf}. These efforts culminated in the 1990s with Okapi BM25, a ranking formula born from Robertson and Sp{\"a}rck Jones' work \sidecite{robertson1994bm25}\sidenote{\textbf{BM25:}
Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a corpus, $d\in\mathcal{D}$ a document, and $t$ a term. The BM25 score of $d$ for a query $q$ is
\[
\mathrm{BM25}(q,d)=
\]
\[
\sum_{t\in q} \mathrm{idf}(t)\;
\frac{f_{t,d}(k_1+1)}{f_{t,d}+k_1\!\left(1-b+b\,\frac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)}
\]
Here $f_{t,d}$ is the count of $t$ in $d$, $|d|$ is the document length (in tokens), and $\mathrm{avgdl}$ is the average document length in the corpus. $k_1>0$ controls term-frequency saturation, and $b\in[0,1]$ controls how strongly length normalization is applied. A common IDF is
\[
\mathrm{idf}(t)=\log\!\left(\frac{N-\mathrm{df}(t)+0.5}{\mathrm{df}(t)+0.5}\right),
\]
where $N=|\mathcal{D}|$ and $\mathrm{df}(t)$ is the number of documents containing $t$.
}. 
By incorporating TF--IDF-like features into a probabilistic model, BM25 became one of the dominant ranking algorithms in search and remains widely used as a strong baseline in modern information retrieval systems \sidecite{robertson2009prf,manning2008introduction_ir}.

Alongside algorithm advances, IR systems grew in scale and importance. The first online search systems appeared in the early 1970s as databases of bibliographic records: for example, MEDLINE (1971) provided biomedical literature search, and DIALOG offered commercial online search over multiple databases \sidecite{nlm2024medline_history,dialog2019milestone}. These systems predated personal computers---users accessed them via terminals---but they demonstrated that searching thousands of documents via text queries was feasible and valuable. In 1978, the IR community established its premier conference, ACM SIGIR, reflecting the field's maturation \sidecite{sigir1978proceedings}.

By the 1980s, personal computing and CD-ROMs enabled standalone IR systems (like library catalog CDs), and researchers explored new ideas such as relevance feedback (the Rocchio algorithm from the 1970s allowed a system to adjust a query based on documents a user marked relevant) \sidecite{rocchio1971relevance_feedback} and clustering (the "cluster hypothesis": relevant documents tend to cluster together).\sidecite{jardine1971hierarchic_clustering,vanrijsbergen1979information_retrieval} The inverted index (storing, for each term, a list of documents containing it) became a core data structure, enabling fast keyword lookup even as collections grew to tens of thousands of documents \sidecite{manning2008introduction_ir}. This era also saw experiments with user interfaces and visualization (e.g.\ plotting document similarity or using hypertext systems).

Evaluation at scale took a leap in 1992 with the start of the Text REtrieval Conference (TREC). TREC built large standard test collections and yearly competitions for state-of-the-art retrieval methods \sidecite{harman1993trec_overview}. TREC not only pushed research towards scalable methods but also fostered the development of specialized tasks. Many techniques that became important, such as query expansion and relevance feedback, were evaluated and refined through TREC workshops.

\section{Enter The Web (1990s--2010s)}

Web search emerged in the mid-1990s as a new frontier that dramatically expanded IR's scale and impact. Early web search systems and directories (for example, Yahoo!'s directory in 1994 and AltaVista in 1995) helped users navigate the growing World Wide Web, but they struggled to rank results in a way that consistently satisfied users at web scale.\sidecite{brin1998anatomy} The breakthrough came in 1998 when Google introduced the PageRank algorithm for ranking web pages.\sidecite{page1998pagerank,brin1998anatomy} PageRank treated the web as a graph, using the pattern of hyperlinks as an additional signal: a page linked from many other pages, especially if those were themselves authoritative, received a higher ``importance'' score.\sidecite{page1998pagerank} By combining link analysis with content relevance, Google achieved much more useful rankings than purely content-based engines.\sidecite{brin1998anatomy,page1998pagerank} This represented a metadata-based retrieval innovation---leveraging network topology (citations) as a relevance signal, analogous to how academic citations indicate influence. PageRank, along with anchor text indexing and other web-specific features, pushed IR beyond just term statistics into using the context of documents (links, click data, etc.) to inform ranking.\sidecite{brin1998anatomy}

In the 2000s, machine learning techniques became increasingly central to IR. Search engines began using learning-to-rank algorithms, which take a large number of features (e.g.\ BM25 scores, document click-through rates, anchor text matches, etc.) and learn how to combine them into a single ranking function.\sidecite{burges2005ranknet,burges2006lambdarank,burges2010lambdamart} For example, researchers at Microsoft developed RankNet and LambdaRank/LambdaMART in the mid-2000s, applying neural networks and boosted decision trees to optimize ranking using labeled training data.\sidecite{burges2005ranknet,burges2006lambdarank,burges2010lambdamart} Commercial engines such as Bing and Google quickly adopted learning-to-rank ideas to tune their search results. By analyzing user behavior signals (e.g.\ which results users click or skip), search engines could implicitly learn relevance.\sidecite{joachims2005clickthrough,radlinski2005query_chains} As a result, 2000s web search saw integration of user click data, query logs, and behavioral analytics to continuously improve results.\sidecite{joachims2005clickthrough,radlinski2005query_chains}

Another 2000s-and-2010s trend was the use of semantic information and knowledge bases. For instance, Bing publicly described its Satori knowledge system in 2013 to recognize entities and relationships in queries.\sidecite{bing2013satori} Both academic and industry efforts moved toward understanding the intent behind queries, not just matching keywords. By 2013, Google's Hummingbird update incorporated more natural language processing to handle conversational queries, marking a shift from pure keyword matching toward semantic context understanding.\sidecite{google2025ranking_systems}

\section{The Deep Learning Era of IR (2010s--2020s)}

The 2010s brought a revolution in information retrieval through neural networks and deep learning. Early in the decade, as deep learning succeeded in speech and vision, researchers began applying neural models to search tasks. Initial approaches like DSSM (Deep Structured Semantic Model) in 2013 used neural networks to map queries and documents into vectors so that relevant pairs had higher similarity.\sidecite{huang2013dssm} These first neural IR models were relatively simple---for example, feed-forward networks over bag-of-word-style or character-trigram representations---but they demonstrated the promise of semantic matching: the ability to retrieve relevant documents even when exact keyword overlap is low.\sidecite{huang2013dssm} For example, a neural model might learn to match ``USA president birthplace'' with documents about ``birthplace of American presidents''---a feat difficult for purely lexical methods.

A major leap came in 2018 when transformer-based language models entered the scene. In 2018, Google researchers introduced BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), a language model that captured rich contextual information about text.\sidecite{devlin2019bert} In October 2019, Google announced that it was applying BERT models to Search for ranking and featured snippets---an early and highly visible deployment of deep neural language models in large-scale web search.\sidecite{google2019bert_search} BERT's ability to model the context of words bidirectionally significantly improved Google's handling of complex queries. Google stated that BERT would help Search better understand about one in ten searches in U.S.\ English, especially longer and more conversational queries where small function words and context matter.\sidecite{google2019bert_search} This deployment helped establish transformer-based NLP as a central component of modern search.\sidecite{yates2021transformers_ranking}

However, BERT was initially used mainly in a re-ranking capacity: the search system would first retrieve a set of candidate documents using traditional methods such as BM25, and then a BERT-based model would score each query--document pair jointly and rerank the candidates with much higher precision.\sidecite{nogueira2019passage_reranking,yates2021transformers_ranking} These cross-encoder models are highly effective because they allow full interaction between the query and document during transformer inference, but they are also computationally expensive---running such a model over every document in a large collection is infeasible for first-stage retrieval.\sidecite{nogueira2019passage_reranking,yates2021transformers_ranking} As a result, a two-stage architecture became common: fast initial retrieval followed by a powerful neural re-ranker for the top $N$ results.\sidecite{yates2021transformers_ranking} Related work such as ColBERT then explored \emph{late interaction} architectures that preserved richer token-level matching than standard bi-encoders while still enabling efficient indexing and retrieval.\sidecite{khattab2020colbert}

Meanwhile, researchers sought ways to make neural retrieval scalable without relying entirely on a two-stage compromise. This gave rise to bi-encoder or dual-encoder models, in which queries and documents are encoded independently into fixed-length embeddings such that relevant pairs have high vector similarity.\sidecite{yates2021transformers_ranking,karpukhin2020dpr} Semantic vector embeddings enable efficient retrieval through nearest-neighbor search in embedding space, using approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) indexes such as HNSW and systems such as FAISS even for very large collections.\sidecite{malkov2020hnsw,johnson2017faiss} A seminal work in this direction was Sentence-BERT (SBERT) in 2019. SBERT fine-tuned BERT in a siamese architecture, using siamese and triplet-network training objectives to produce semantically meaningful sentence embeddings that can be compared directly with cosine similarity.\sidecite{reimers2019sbert} This approach drastically improved the practicality of text embeddings for semantic search: instead of applying expensive cross-attention to every sentence pair, an IR system could pre-compute document embeddings and answer queries through fast vector similarity search.\sidecite{reimers2019sbert} The authors reported a dramatic speedup on a 10,000-sentence similarity search task---from roughly 65 hours with vanilla BERT / RoBERTa pairwise inference to about 5 seconds with SBERT.\sidecite{reimers2019sbert}

SBERT helped trigger an explosion of dense retrieval research. In 2020, Facebook AI introduced DPR (Dense Passage Retrieval) for open-domain question answering, training dual BERT encoders for questions and passages such that relevant pairs had higher dot-product similarity than irrelevant ones.\sidecite{karpukhin2020dpr} DPR and related models showed that dense retrieval could outperform strong BM25 baselines on several passage retrieval benchmarks while remaining practical at scale.\sidecite{karpukhin2020dpr} Early sentence-embedding work often used siamese or triplet-style objectives, as in SBERT,\sidecite{reimers2019sbert} but by 2020--2021 many influential dense retrieval and embedding methods were using contrastive softmax objectives with in-batch negatives, closely related to the InfoNCE family of losses.\sidecite{karpukhin2020dpr,oord2018cpc,gao2021simcse} By 2021, SimCSE showed that even unsupervised contrastive learning---using different dropout realizations of the same sentence as positive pairs---could produce powerful sentence embeddings that rivaled earlier supervised approaches.\sidecite{gao2021simcse} This trend helped solidify contrastive learning as a central training paradigm for modern text embeddings and dense retrieval.\sidecite{karpukhin2020dpr,gao2021simcse}
