\section{This Thesis in a Glance} 


Deep neural networks used to index and query information rely on a semantic understanding of the content to retrieve documents at large scales, departing from previous methods that leveraged meta-information (title, author, tags, document visits), or relied on statistical heuristics to match content to a user query (keyword matching). This thesis argues AI-powered search systems are still in their infancy, and can be greatly improved, notably by leveraging easily available yet often overlooked information (priors). 

\subsection{Part 1: Visual Document Retrieval} 

Efforts in deep semantic search have to this day mostly focused on creating meaningful latent representation spaces to map short text passages to their equivalent latent representations - embeddings - in a manifold that aligns semantical proximity with geometric distance. We show that in practice, restricting search to this setup is quite restrictive and many non-standard sources of information can be explored to improve the performance of search systems across a wider array of situations. In the first part of this manuscript, our investigations concentrate on leveraging previously untapped existing knowledge ("priors") to enhance the performance of deep learning based information retrieval systems. 

This thesis notably introduces the concept of \textit{Visual Document Retrieval} (VDR) through the publication "ColPali: Efficient Document Retrieval with Vision Language Models" \sidecite{faysse2025colpaliefficientdocumentretrieval}, which first proposes doing document retrieval without relying on text extracted from document pages, but rather by indexing pages and all semantic content represented through the page image (the pixels) directly. We show this enables greatly simplifying and speeding up document indexing pipelines while also greatly improving retrieval performance, notably on visually complex documents such as those containing tables, figures, complex layouts... 

The success of VDR has led since the original publication to many follow up works by academic and industrial actors. As a result, model performances rapidly improved, saturating the original Visual Document Retrieval benchmark \textit{ViDoRe} introduced in the ColPali paper, and leading to subsequent benchmarking efforts mentioned rapidly in this thesis in chapter XXX. In ViDoRe V2 \sidecite{macé2025vidorebenchmarkv2raising}, we construct complex and realistic pairs of queries and documents to assess retrieval performance on open-ended questions spanning up to multiple pages and rely on complex AI pipelines to simulate realistic interactions between users and information corpora. In ViDoRe V3, we collaborate with Nvidia, to further scale annotation efforts on varied domains reflecting real world use of industrial search systems and develop an annotation pipeline that combines AI agents with over 1000 hours of expert human annotators.

A year after the publication of the ColPali paper, we released "ModernVBert", a visual document retrieval model that commoditizes VDR performance by showcasing similar performances than the original ColPali model while being 10x smaller and running on CPU \sidecite{teiletche2025modernvbert}. This was made possible by through ablations of all performance factors behind vision language retrieval models and many critical insights were uncovered and detailed in chapter XX.

A limitation of Visual Document Retrieval models are their limited ability to leverage contextual information found in previous document pages. This shortcoming is in fact not inherent to visual retrievers, but is also a major weakness of the more common textual embedding models which embed long documents as a series of independant passages, without exploiting the fact to a reader, certain passages might be key to understand others. We quantify the impact of context aware retrieval and propose technical solutions based on late-chunking aware training in chapter X \sidecite{conti2025context}. While this work is instanciated on textual retrievers for ease of experimentation, it is directly applicable to our work on improving visual retrievers.

The contributions of this thesis go beyond a series of peer reviewed publications. Significant efforts were devoted to continuously improve the performance, to extend the capabilities and to advocate for the use of Visual Document Retrieval models. We can notably mention the release of the ColQwen model series\sidenote{We published the models openly and integrated the code on HuggingFace \url{https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/main/model_doc/colqwen2}}, a series of SOTA visual retrievers, downloaded over 5 million times on HuggingFace at the time of writing. We also extended retrieval to other modalities such as video and audio through ColQwen-Omni \sidenote{We published the model openly on the HuggingFace platform and released a blogpost at \url{https://huggingface.co/blog/manu/colqwen-omni-omnimodal-retrieval}.} and maintained the ColPali GitHub library at \url{https://github.com/illuin-tech/colpali}. The field of Visual Document Retrieval is now well established and thriving.



\subsection{Part 2: Beyond Embedding-Based Retrieval}

In the second part of this thesis, we turn to the most recent evolution of search engines: systems that are no longer designed primarily for direct human use, but instead operate \emph{through} AI agents---in particular, Large Language Models (LLMs). Progress in this direction spans a wide set of components and research threads. In this thesis, we narrow our attention to a sequence of projects whose shared goal is to improve the trustworthiness of these generative systems. As the burden of search is increasingly delegated from humans to LLM-based agents, trust must be earned in other ways: through transparency, careful system design, and rigorous capability evaluation. Although the work presented in this part is not always framed explicitly as ``search'', strong LLMs are a central building block for the next phase of AI-powered retrieval and interaction, often described as \emph{agentic search}. The overarching objective is to enable machine-learning-powered search tools that are both more capable and more reliable.

In Chapter~X, we introduce CroissantLLM \sidecite{faysse2024croissantllm}, a compact bilingual French--English language model trained on an equal mixture of English and French data. Initiated in 2023, this project was among the first open efforts to highlight the benefits of training with high token-to-parameter ratios, and among the early attempts to train sub-7B models \emph{outside} of a larger scaling ladder with the explicit goal of delivering strong performance at small size for efficient inference. At release time, CroissantLLM achieved state-of-the-art results on French language benchmarks and matched the strongest English models in its size category. It also delivered particularly strong performance on English--French translation, often surpassing models that were orders of magnitude larger. Beyond the model itself, the CroissantLLM project emphasizes the importance of bilingual training corpora, establishes joint scaling laws for bilingual settings, and is accompanied by substantial data work: the release of high-quality French resources reused in many subsequent projects, as well as new French benchmarks. Models, code, datasets, and evaluation suites are released publicly, supporting continued progress toward strong local models that run efficiently on consumer hardware.

In Chapter~X, we motivate a shift in evaluation practice: using LLMs themselves as judges for capability assessment. While this approach is now widespread, the 2023 paper ``Revisiting Instruction Model Evaluation'' \sidenote{Written in May 2023 and released in October 2023 after the EMNLP anonymity period, following the rules in place at the time.} \sidecite{faysse2023revisiting} was an early work advocating the use of LLM-based evaluation for broad capability measurement. The paper stems from the observation that instruction-tuned LLMs are deployed across an increasingly diverse set of tasks, making task-specific metrics difficult to maintain, poorly calibrated, and ultimately non-scalable. It shows that LLM judges can provide consistent signal across heterogeneous tasks and remain robust to variation in expected answer formats---settings where many traditional generative metrics fall short. The analysis further reveals distinct phases during supervised fine-tuning: models often learn to match the \emph{format} of the desired output before improving underlying task performance. Building on this finding, the work proposes leveraging synthetic data augmentation to improve data efficiency. Overall, this chapter argues for a fairer and more practical assessment of the capabilities of this new generation of models. Since the release of this work, LLM-as-a-judge methods have become a dominant approach for evaluating RAG systems and related techniques \sidecite{es2025ragasautomatedevaluationretrieval, kim2023prometheus, muller2025grousebenchmarkevaluateevaluators}.

They focus on \emph{training-data memorization} and \emph{membership inference} in large language models: to what extent are individual training examples memorized, and how can one provide evidence that a given text was included in an LLM's training corpus? These questions have direct relevance to ongoing legal disputes and raise broad policy implications, notably around copyright and data governance. Together, these contributions move toward more rigorous and auditable assessments of modern LLM behavior, and offer practical tools to reason about privacy and provenance in large-scale training pipelines.

\subsection{Conclusion: On the future of search}
Tying back to the central theme of search, the conclusion outlines what the next generation of information access may look like. We argue that \emph{omnimodal} search will become central as the set of exploitable sources of information widens with model capabilites (text, images, audio, video, code, structured data, 3D models).
Recent projects such as BrowseComp illustrate the growing impact of AI agents on real-world information seeking and highlight that robust access to complex information benefits from \emph{adaptive compute}. Crucially, these systems still benefit from strong ``first-layer'' retrieval primitives such as embedding-based search for efficient candidate selection.

The conclusion further reflects on the fact that verifying search correctness is not uniformly easy. Instead, search exhibits a \emph{jagged frontier} of capabilities: some tasks (e.g., factual verification grounded in accessible digital sources) are comparatively straightforward to check and should steadily improve \sidenote{Although as demonstrated by Vidore V3, complex queries remain very lengthy to verify even for human experts.}, whereas underspecified requests, open-ended synthesis, and value-laden questions remain harder both to solve and to verify. In this lens, verifier-side constraints shape what kinds of search problems will be reliably improved ``verifier's law''. (Mention the fact a lot of weak signals are provided by the user in a tyoical search system and can be exploited to understand whether a system has satisified a user request (are they asking follow ups, etc).

We further note that not all information is equally accessible: some sources remain costly to reach, poorly indexed, private, ephemeral... Exploiting such information at scale will therefore remain challenging in the coming years. Looking forward, we anticipate a shift from explicit querying toward more \emph{implicit} information access, where systems proactively surface relevant knowledge continuously in response to context, goals, and ongoing activity.

Finally, we argue that long-context modeling and information retrieval are closer than they may appear, and that bridging these literatures can be fruitful: both fields study how to select, compress, and condition on relevant evidence, finding needles of relevamnt information in a haystack of noise. Building conceptual and methodological links between them may be possible.

Beyond the technical horizon, we close by reflecting on what truly frictionless information access would mean, and on the societal implications of delegating discovery, synthesis, and decision support to increasingly agentic systems.

\chapter{A Brief History of Information Retrieval}

While this thesis does not aim to provide an extensive overview of past or modern Information Retrieval techniques, the following section will provide a rapid overview of the field in order to contextualize the contributions of this thesis\sidenote{As a disclaimer, this chapter is the only chapter of this thesis in which generative AI tools were used to assist redaction. All content is thoroughly verified.}.


\section{Ancient and Early Pre-Digital Cataloging}

Long before computers, humans developed methods to organize and retrieve information. One of the earliest examples is the Pinakes of Callimachus around the 3rd century BCE, often considered the first library catalog in the Western world. The Pinakes systematically listed authors and their works in the Library of Alexandria, grouping works by subject and alphabetizing authors – a bibliographic approach that laid the groundwork for later library catalogs. This ancient effort demonstrated the enduring principles of indexing and categorization (such as alphabetical order and subject grouping) that remain fundamental in information retrieval (IR).
Through the centuries, librarians and scholars continued to develop catalogs and classification systems (e.g. medieval manuscript indexes and Melvil Dewey’s Decimal Classification in 1876). These pre-digital innovations weren’t “IR systems” in the modern sense, but they addressed the same core challenge: how to store, organize, and find relevant information efficiently. By the early 20th century, visionaries like Paul Otlet dreamed of organizing the world’s knowledge (Otlet’s Mundaneum project) using index cards – a proto-IR system. In 1945, Vannevar Bush articulated a futuristic vision of personal information devices in As We May Think, describing the Memex – a hypothetical electromechanical desk that could instantly retrieve microfilmed documents via associative links. Bush’s essay inspired later developments by suggesting that managing the “growing mountain of information” would require new mechanized solutions.

\section{Founding of Information Retrieval (1950s–1960s)}
IR as a distinct field took shape in the 1950s and 1960s, alongside the advent of digital computers. In 1950, Calvin Mooers coined the term “information retrieval”, heralding the idea of using machines to search document collections. Early experiments followed quickly: in 1951, a master’s thesis at MIT by Philip Bagley demonstrated one of the first computerized document retrieval experiments. Pioneers like Hans Peter Luhn at IBM began applying computers to automate indexing; for example, Luhn developed methods for auto-encoding documents and keyword-in-context indexing. By 1959 he published an influential paper on “Auto-encoding of documents for information retrieval,” showing how computers could encode text and allow electronic searching of content. This period also saw growing support for IR research as the U.S. government recognized an information explosion (and a “science gap”), funding projects on mechanized literature searching and the creation of citation indexes.
A key milestone was the Cranfield experiments led by C. W. “Pete” Cleverdon in the late 1950s and early 1960s. The Cranfield studies introduced a rigorous experimental methodology for IR: assembling a test collection of documents, queries, and relevance judgments, and evaluating retrieval performance using metrics like precision and recall. Cleverdon’s 1962 report on Cranfield showed how different indexing techniques could be objectively compared. This methodology – the use of standardized test collections and metrics – became a cornerstone of IR research, establishing a scientific approach to evaluate how “relevant” the results returned by an IR system are[. The Cranfield approach was hard work (manually judging relevance for many query-document pairs), but it laid the foundation for future benchmarks.


During the same era, Gerard Salton formed the first major IR research group (at Harvard, later Cornell). Researchers began formulating the first theoretical models of IR. In 1960, Maron and Kuhns proposed a probabilistic indexing model, foreshadowing later probabilistic retrieval approaches. Salton and others started developing what became the vector space model: by the late 1960s, Salton’s team had created the SMART system, representing documents and queries as vectors in a term-weighted space. In 1968 Salton published “Automatic Information Organization and Retrieval,” and by 1975 he fully articulated the vector space framework and term-weighting schemes. Another 1960s contribution came from Karen Spärck Jones, who introduced the concept of inverse document frequency (IDF) (published in 1972) to statistically downweight common terms and boost rare, informative terms – a principle that soon became central to ranking algorithms.


\section{The Statistical Revolution in IR (1970s–1990s)}

With the fundamentals laid, subsequent decades saw rapid improvement in statistical text retrieval methods. The 1970s refined the vector space and probabilistic models. Term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF–IDF) weighting became a standard technique: terms are scored higher if they appear frequently in a document (term frequency) but are rare across the collection (high IDF)\sidenote{
\textbf{TF-IDF:}
Let $\mathcal{D}=\{d_1,\dots,d_N\}$ be a corpus of $N$ documents and let $t$ be a term (token).
The TF--IDF weight of term $t$ in document $d$ is
\[
\mathrm{tfidf}(t,d,\mathcal{D}) \;=\; \mathrm{tf}(t,d)\cdot \mathrm{idf}(t,\mathcal{D}).
\]
A common choice is
\[
\mathrm{tf}(t,d)=\frac{f_{t,d}}{\sum_{t'} f_{t',d}}
\]
and
\[
\mathrm{idf}(t,\mathcal{D})=\log\!\left(\frac{N}{\mathrm{df}(t)}\right),
\]
where $f_{t,d}$ is the raw count of occurrences of $t$ in $d$, $\sum_{t'} f_{t',d}$ is the total number of term occurrences in $d$ (so $\mathrm{tf}$ is a normalized frequency),
and $\mathrm{df}(t)=\bigl|\{d_i\in\mathcal{D}: t\in d_i\}\bigr|$ is the document frequency (the number of documents containing $t$ at least once).
Intuitively, $\mathrm{tf}$ upweights terms frequent in a given document, while $\mathrm{idf}$ downweights terms that appear in many documents.
(Variants often use $\mathrm{tf}(t,d)=1+\log f_{t,d}$ and/or smoothing $\log\!\left(\frac{N+1}{\mathrm{df}(t)+1}\right)+1$ to avoid division by zero.)
}. Salton’s publications in 1975 detailed how weighting schemes like TF–IDF could significantly improve retrieval effectiveness[. On the probabilistic side, Stephen Robertson and colleagues extended early ideas into the Probabilistic Relevance Framework (1970s–80s), leading to the development of ranking functions that estimate the probability a document is relevant to a query. These efforts culminated in the 1990s with Okapi BM25, a ranking formula born from Robertson and Spärck Jones’ work\sidenote{\textbf{BM25:}
Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a corpus, $d\in\mathcal{D}$ a document, and $t$ a term. The BM25 score of $d$ for a query $q$ is
\[
\mathrm{BM25}(q,d)=\sum_{t\in q} \mathrm{idf}(t)\;
\frac{f_{t,d}(k_1+1)}{f_{t,d}+k_1\!\left(1-b+b\,\frac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)}.
\]
Here $f_{t,d}$ is the count of $t$ in $d$, $|d|$ is the document length (in tokens), and $\mathrm{avgdl}$ is the average document length in the corpus. $k_1>0$ controls term-frequency saturation, and $b\in[0,1]$ controls how strongly length normalization is applied. A common IDF is
\[
\mathrm{idf}(t)=\log\!\left(\frac{N-\mathrm{df}(t)+0.5}{\mathrm{df}(t)+0.5}\right),
\]
where $N=|\mathcal{D}|$ and $\mathrm{df}(t)$ is the number of documents containing $t$.
}. 
BM25 operationalized probabilistic retrieval in a simple yet powerful way: it computes a relevance score based on term frequencies and document length normalization, along with an IDF component. By incorporating TF–IDF-like features into a probabilistic model, BM25 became the de facto ranking algorithm in many search engines by the late 1990s. In fact, BM25 (and variants) remain widely used as a strong baseline – it’s “the base of modern information retrieval” in many applications.


Alongside algorithm advances, IR systems grew in scale and importance. The first online search systems appeared in the early 1970s as databases of bibliographic records: for example, MEDLINE (1971) provided biomedical literature search, and Dialog (1972, by Lockheed) offered commercial online search over multiple databases. These systems predated personal computers – users accessed them via terminals – but they demonstrated that searching thousands of documents via text queries was feasible and valuable. In 1978, the IR community established its premier conference, ACM SIGIR, reflecting the field’s maturation.


By the 1980s, personal computing and CD-ROMs enabled standalone IR systems (like library catalog CDs), and researchers explored new ideas such as relevance feedback (the Rocchio algorithm from the 1970s allowed a system to adjust a query based on documents a user marked relevant) and clustering (“cluster hypothesis”: relevant documents tend to cluster together). The inverted index (storing, for each term, a list of documents containing it) became a core data structure, enabling fast keyword lookup even as collections grew to tens of thousands of documents. This era also saw experiments with user interfaces and visualization (e.g. plotting document similarity or using hypertext systems).
Evaluation at scale took a leap in 1992 with the start of the Text Retrieval Conference (TREC). TREC, organized by NIST and Dept. of Defense, built large standard test collections (hundreds of thousands of documents) and yearly competitions for state-of-the-art retrieval methods. TREC not only pushed research towards scalable methods but also fostered the development of specialized tasks (e.g. web, QA, filtering). Many techniques that became important (like query expansion, using thesauri or relevance feedback to add related terms to a query) were evaluated and refined through TREC workshops.


Web search emerged in the mid-1990s as a new frontier that dramatically expanded IR’s scale and impact. Early web search engines (e.g. Yahoo! in 1994, AltaVista in 1995) primarily used traditional keyword matching (similar to vector space or Boolean models), but they struggled to rank results in a way that satisfied users on the vast World Wide Web. The breakthrough came in 1998 when Google introduced the PageRank algorithm for ranking web pages. PageRank treated the web as a graph, using the pattern of hyperlinks as an additional signal: a page linked from many other pages, especially if those were themselves authoritative, received a higher “importance” score. By combining link analysis with content relevance, Google achieved much more useful rankings than purely content-based engines. This represented a metadata-based retrieval innovation – leveraging network topology (citations) as a relevance signal, analogous to how academic citations indicate influence. PageRank, along with anchor text indexing and other web-specific features, pushed IR beyond just term statistics into using the context of documents (links, click data, etc.) to inform ranking.


In the 2000s, machine learning techniques became increasingly central to IR. Search engines began using learning-to-rank algorithms, which take a large number of features (e.g. BM25 scores, document click-through rates, anchor text matches, etc.) and learn how to combine them into a single ranking function. For example, researchers at Microsoft developed RankNet and LambdaRank/MART in the mid-2000s, applying neural networks and boosted decision trees to optimize ranking using labeled training data. Commercial engines like Bing and Google quickly adopted these to tune their search results. By analyzing user behavior signals (e.g. which results users click or skip), search engines could implicitly learn relevance. As a result, 2000s web search saw integration of user click data, query logs, and behavioral analytics to continuously improve results.
Another 2000s trend was the use of semantic information and knowledge bases. For instance, in 2009 Bing introduced the Satori knowledge base to recognize entities and relationships in queries. Both academic and industry efforts moved toward understanding the intent behind queries, not just matching keywords. By 2013, Google’s “Hummingbird” update incorporated more natural language processing to handle conversational queries, marking a shift from pure keyword matching toward semantic context understanding.


\section{The Deep Learning Era of IR (2010s–2020s)}
The 2010s brought a revolution in information retrieval through neural networks and deep learning. Early in the decade, as deep learning succeeded in speech and vision, researchers began applying neural models to search tasks. Initial approaches like DSSM (Deep Structured Semantic Model) in 2013 used neural networks to map queries and documents into vectors so that relevant pairs had higher similarity. These first neural IR models were relatively simple (e.g. feed-forward networks on bag-of-word or character-trigram inputs), but they demonstrated the promise of semantic matching: the ability to retrieve relevant documents even when exact keyword overlap is low. For example, a neural model might learn to match “USA president birthplace” with documents about “birthplace of American presidents” – a feat difficult for purely lexical methods.
A major leap came in 2018 when transformer models entered the scene. In late 2018, Google researchers released BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), a language model that captured rich contextual understanding of text. Shortly after, Google deployed BERT in its search engine – one of the first instances of a deep neural language model used at scale in real-world retrieval. BERT’s ability to understand natural language queries (by considering the context of words bidirectionally) significantly improved Google’s handling of complex queries. For example, BERT helped the engine grasp nuances like stop-words or word sense in queries (“train to Chicago” vs “train a model”), improving relevance for about 10\% of searches by Google’s estimate. This deployment proved that neural networks could outperform hand-tuned ranking formulas for certain query types, especially on conversational or natural language queries. It also heralded a broader adoption of NLP models in search.


However, BERT was initially used in a re-ranking capacity: the search system would first retrieve a set of candidate documents using traditional methods (keywords/BM25), then a BERT-based cross-encoder would analyze each query–document pair and produce a relevance score, reranking the candidates with much improved precision. Cross-encoders (so-called “full interaction” models) examine the query and document together with the full attention mechanism of transformers, yielding very accurate relevance judgments. The downside is computational cost – running BERT for every document in a large collection is infeasible for first-round retrieval. Thus, a two-stage approach became common: a fast initial retrieval (e.g. BM25) followed by a powerful neural re-ranker like BERT for the top N results.


Meanwhile, researchers sought ways to make neural retrieval scalable without the two-stage compromise. This gave rise to bi-encoder or dual-encoder models, where queries and documents are each encoded independently into a fixed-length embedding, such that relevant pairs have high vector similarity. Semantic vector embeddings enable efficient retrieval via nearest neighbor search in embedding space, which can be done with specialized indexes (like ANN – Approximate Nearest Neighbors algorithms) even for millions of vectors. A seminal work in this direction was Sentence-BERT (SBERT) in 2019. SBERT fine-tuned BERT in a siamese network architecture to directly produce meaningful sentence embeddings, using paired sentences labeled by semantic similarity for training. This approach – essentially training BERT to output a single vector for a sentence such that similar sentences map to nearby vectors – drastically improved the quality of text embeddings. SBERT’s embeddings were far more semantically meaningful than averaging BERT’s word vectors, and importantly, made it practical to compare sentences via cosine similarity instead of expensive cross-attention. The authors demonstrated massive speedups for semantic search: finding the closest matches among 10,000 sentences took seconds with SBERT, versus hours if one had to run BERT on each pair. In essence, SBERT allowed IR systems to pre-compute embeddings for all documents and then handle queries by a quick vector lookup, enabling real-time semantic search on sizable corpora.

SBERT kicked off an explosion of dense retrieval research. In 2020, Facebook AI applied a similar idea for open-domain question answering with DPR (Dense Passage Retrieval): they trained dual BERT encoders for questions and passages such that correct Q–A pairs had higher dot-product than irrelevant pairs. DPR (and its contemporaries) used contrastive learning: given a query, the model was trained to output an embedding closer to its true relevant passage than to various negative passages. Initially, many used triplet loss or hinge loss (as SBERT did) on these triplets of (query, positive, negative). Soon, the field shifted to the now-standard InfoNCE loss (a form of cross-entropy over a batch that treats one positive pair vs. all other negatives), which effectively enables in-batch negatives and better gradient signal. By 2021, methods like SimCSE demonstrated that even unsupervised contrastive learning (using different dropout versions of the same sentence as positive pairs) could yield powerful sentence embeddings, rivaling supervised ones. This trend solidified contrastive objectives as the go-to training method for text embeddings, replacing earlier triplet formulations with a simpler and more robust approach (Noise-Contrastive Estimation).
